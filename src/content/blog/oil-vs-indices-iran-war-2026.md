On August 6, 2026, I [ran the numbers on oil and the S&P 500](/blog/oil-equities-iran-war-2026) since the February 28 Iran strikes and found a real but partial relationship — a −0.50 Pearson correlation on daily returns, fading to −0.37 by Phase 3. The S&P 500 is one number, though, and it's an average of nearly 500 companies that don't all have the same relationship with oil. Split the same question across the Dow, the Nasdaq 100, and the Nasdaq Composite, and the average stops looking like a fair summary. Over the full period, the Dow's correlation with oil (−0.62) is more than 70% stronger than the Nasdaq 100's (−0.36) — and by Phase 3, that gap has widened to more than double, because the Dow's relationship with oil isn't shrinking the way the other three indices' are.

Same setup as last time: Pearson correlation of daily percentage returns (not price levels), oil via WTI (CL=F, yfinance), computed for the full period and the same three phases — active conflict (Feb 28–Apr 7), the ceasefire lull (Apr 8–May 6), and the second escalation (May 7–Aug 5) — this time against the Dow (^DJI), Nasdaq 100 (^NDX), and Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC).

## What actually happened, index by index

The chart makes the split obvious before you even get to the correlation math. All three indices fell together in the first weeks of the war — the Dow bottomed at index 92.2 on March 27, the Nasdaq 100 and Nasdaq Composite both bottomed around index 92.0 and 91.7 on March 30 — and by April 7, the day before the ceasefire, none of them had recovered to their pre-war level. Oil, meanwhile, was already up 68.5% that same day. That gap — equities still underwater while oil was deep in overshoot territory — is the first sign that these are genuinely different trades, not one trade at different speeds.

After the ceasefire, the paths diverge hard. As of August 5: the Nasdaq 100 is up 18.1% since February 27, the Nasdaq Composite is up 16.3%, the S&P 500 is up 12.3%, and the Dow is up just 11.0% — a full 7-point gap between the best and worst performer, on the same war, over the same five months. Some of that Nasdaq strength has nothing to do with Iran at all. The Nasdaq had its own correction in July — chip stocks, circular AI financing, a hawkish Fed vote, [covered in more detail here](/blog/nasdaq-correction-august-2026) — that played out entirely inside this window and would have happened in a world with no war at all. That's the point worth sitting with before the correlation numbers: the Nasdaq-heavy indices have a large, genuinely independent story running underneath this whole period. The Dow doesn't have an equivalent.

## The correlation, phase by phase, index by index

- **Full period (Mar 2–Aug 5, n=109):** Dow −0.62 · S&P 500 −0.50 · Nasdaq Composite −0.43 · Nasdaq 100 −0.36.
- **Phase 1 — active conflict (n=26):** Dow −0.55 · S&P 500 −0.48 · Nasdaq Composite −0.46 · Nasdaq 100 −0.46.
- **Phase 2 — ceasefire lull (n=21):** S&P 500 −0.80 · Nasdaq Composite −0.77 · Dow −0.77 · Nasdaq 100 −0.74.
- **Phase 3 — second escalation (n=62):** Dow −0.56 · S&P 500 −0.37 · Nasdaq Composite −0.30 · Nasdaq 100 −0.22.

Phase 2 confirms what the S&P piece already found: during the ceasefire, when the only question the market was trading was whether the truce would hold, every index moved with oil at close to the same strength (−0.74 to −0.80). One dominant narrative produces one tight correlation, and that held across all four indices, not just the S&P. It's Phase 3 where the story breaks apart. In the S&P piece, I read the drop from −0.48 to −0.37 as equities decoupling from the war as the Fed and AI-driven liquidity took over as the bigger story. That holds for the Nasdaq 100, where the correlation more than halved (−0.46 to −0.22), and for the Nasdaq Composite (−0.46 to −0.30) — if anything, the decoupling there is sharper than the S&P showed. It does not hold for the Dow. Its Phase 3 correlation, −0.56, is essentially unchanged from Phase 1's −0.55. Whatever caused the Nasdaq to stop trading on oil never happened to the Dow.

## Why composition explains it — and it's not the reason you'd guess

The obvious explanation is that the Dow has more energy exposure, so of course it tracks oil more closely. That's not really it, and the actual mechanism is more interesting.

Chevron is the Dow's only major oil producer, and it carries roughly a 2.2% weight in the index — a below-average weight for one of its 30 components ([StockMarketWatch](https://stockmarketwatch.com/indices/dowjones/reports/july-2026)). That's not because Chevron is small; it's because the Dow is price-weighted, not market-cap-weighted, so a stock's influence on the index depends on its nominal share price, not the size of the company behind it. According to State Street's official fact sheet for the Dow-tracking DIA ETF (as of June 30, 2026), the two largest weights in the index are Caterpillar at 12.32% and Goldman Sachs at 11.70% — together almost a quarter of the entire index — purely because their shares trade at high nominal prices, not because they're the Dow's biggest or most systemically important companies ([State Street SPDR DIA Fact Sheet](https://www.ssga.com/library-content/products/factsheets/etfs/us/factsheet-us-en-dia.pdf)). Microsoft, despite being one of the most valuable companies on earth, carries only a 4.32% weight in the Dow for the same structural reason; Amazon isn't even in the top ten. So the Dow's tilt toward oil isn't really an energy-sector story. It's that its two heaviest-weighted names are an industrial-cyclical machinery maker and a financial mega-cap whose own earnings — trading revenue, underwriting, M&A fees — move with broad market volatility and macro conditions rather than against them. Neither is a long-duration growth stock with a narrative powerful enough to run independently of the news cycle, and between them they set the tone for almost a quarter of the index. Industrial-cyclical and market-sensitive-financial names both trade on the same global-growth-and-risk-sentiment axis that oil headlines move, whether or not they touch energy directly.

Compare that to the Nasdaq 100, where the top five holdings — Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, and Alphabet — make up roughly 30% of the index by themselves, and the top ten hold more than 35% ([TradingSim](https://www.tradingsim.com/blog/10-best-holdings-in-qqq-stock)). That's about as concentrated a bet on one narrative as a major index gets, and the narrative is AI capital spending and the Fed's rate path, not the Strait of Hormuz. The site's post on the July correction made the case that AI and other high-multiple growth names are the part of the market most exposed to the Fed's rate path, precisely because so much of their expected cash flow sits years out. That same duration exposure cuts both ways here: it means Nasdaq 100 constituents have been trading primarily on Fed policy and AI-earnings expectations throughout 2026, which crowds out however much daily variance oil would otherwise explain. The Nasdaq Composite sits in between the Dow and the Nasdaq 100 for a similarly structural reason: it holds the same mega-cap core as the Nasdaq 100, but spreads it across more than 3,200 listings — 3,269 as of January 2025, the last published count ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/nasdaq-composite-154127577.html)) — a long tail of smaller, more cyclically exposed tech and non-tech names that dilute the AI-narrative concentration just enough to let more of oil's influence back in. Its correlation numbers mostly bear that out: over the full period and in Phase 3, it sits between the Nasdaq 100's and the Dow's, as you'd expect from an index that's part mega-cap core and part cyclical tail. In the ceasefire lull, when one dominant narrative pulled every index into near-identical alignment, it landed essentially tied with the Dow instead — a reminder that these structural tendencies are second-order compared to whatever single story the whole market happens to be trading that week.

## What this says about how risk is actually being priced

Here's my real position: the S&P 500's −0.37 in Phase 3 isn't a market-wide verdict that this war has become background noise. It's an average of two markets that have reached opposite conclusions, and averaging them together makes the picture look like measured, uniform caution when it's actually a split decision.

The Nasdaq 100 has, in practice, stopped pricing this war as anything more than a source of noisy daily volatility — its correlation with oil in Phase 3 is the weakest of any index here, and its total return since February 27 is the strongest, war notwithstanding. That's not recklessness; it's a mechanical consequence of a 100-stock index whose top five names run on a capital-spending and interest-rate story with far more daily weight than anything happening in the Gulf. The Dow hasn't reached that conclusion, and structurally can't — it has no equivalent counterweight sitting at the top of its weighting scheme, so more of its day-to-day movement stays attributable to the same macro and geopolitical sentiment that's been driving oil since February. If you want an honest read on whether markets still think this war carries real economic risk, the Dow is a better place to look than the S&P, and a far better place than the Nasdaq. It's the only one of the four indices in this piece whose relationship with oil never loosened.

That split is also the thing I'd watch going forward, more than the war itself. The Nasdaq's insulation is borrowed from its own narrative, not from any judgment about Iran — it holds only as long as the Fed path and AI capex story stay intact. If either of those cracks at the same time the Strait is still contested, there's nothing left diluting oil's influence in the Nasdaq numbers, and I'd expect its correlation to snap toward the Dow's level, not the other way around.
