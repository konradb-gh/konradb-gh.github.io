A dividend is a portion of a company's profit paid out directly to shareholders, usually in cash, just for owning the stock. There's nothing to do to earn it beyond holding shares on the right date — no extra action, no fine print to fill out.

## Apple's dividend, in real numbers

Apple pays $0.27 per share every quarter, declared most recently on July 30, 2026 — $1.08 a year across four payments ([Dividend Investor](https://www.dividendinvestor.com/dividend-news/20260730/apple-inc-nasdaq-aapl-declared-a-dividend-of-$0.2700-per-share/)). Own 100 shares — a round, ordinary position, not a whale-sized one — and that's $27 landing in your account every three months, $108 for the year, on top of whatever the stock price itself does. You don't have to sell anything or time anything. You just have to already own the shares before a specific cutoff, which is where the two dates that actually matter come in.

## Ex-dividend date and record date

The **record date** is the date the company checks its own books to see who's officially a shareholder — and therefore owed the dividend. The **ex-dividend date** (or "ex-date") is the first date a share trades *without* the dividend attached: buy on or after it, and the dividend goes to whoever sold it to you, not you.

For that same Apple dividend, both dates fell on August 10, 2026, with payment on August 13 ([Dividend Investor](https://www.dividendinvestor.com/dividend-news/20260730/apple-inc-nasdaq-aapl-declared-a-dividend-of-$0.2700-per-share/)). Same day — which is worth pausing on, because it hasn't always worked that way, and the reason it changed is entirely about how long a trade takes to settle.

When you buy a stock, you don't own it, on the company's books, the instant you click buy — the trade needs to *settle* first. In the US, that settlement period is one business day (T+1) as of May 2024, down from the two business days (T+2) it took before. To be the registered owner by record date R, your trade has to settle by R, which means you had to buy by R minus one business day at the latest. That makes R itself the first day a *new* buyer can't get there in time — which is exactly the definition of the ex-date. Under the old T+2 rule, that same math pushed the ex-date to a full business day *before* the record date, since a trade needed two days to settle instead of one ([StockTitan](https://www.stocktitan.net/articles/t-plus-1-settlement-explained)). Shorten settlement, and the gap between the two dates shrinks right along with it — which is precisely what happened to Apple's own dividend dates.

## Why the price usually dips on the ex-date

On the morning of the ex-date, exchanges mechanically adjust the stock's reference price down by roughly the dividend amount — a $50 stock paying a $0.50 dividend opens with its reference price, and any outstanding limit orders, marked down to about $49.50 ([Schwab](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/ex-dividend-dates-understanding-dividend-risk)). That's a bookkeeping adjustment, not a guarantee about where the stock actually trades that day. Real buyers and sellers still set the price from there — good news, a market-wide rally, or just ordinary noise can easily push the stock well above or below that adjusted starting point by the closing bell. The exchange resets the reference point; the market decides what happens next.

## The same relationship, running on different clocks

Because the ex-date/record-date gap is just a function of settlement time, it looks different in every market that hasn't moved to the same settlement speed at the same pace — and this has genuinely been in motion the last few years, not a fixed fact to memorize once and forget.

**The US**, as above, moved to T+1 in May 2024. Ex-date and record date now generally land on the same day.

**India** got there even earlier, moving to T+1 back in 2023. The pattern is the same: a real 2026 example is BSE Ltd's ₹10-per-share dividend, where the record date and ex-date both fell on July 10, 2026 ([Zee Business](https://www.zeebiz.com/market-news/news-500-dividend-payout-bse-dividend-record-date-next-week-heres-the-last-day-to-buy-shares-398353)).

**The UK** hasn't made the jump yet. It's still on T+2 as of today, which is why FTSE 100 shares still show the older pattern — an ex-date on a Thursday typically paired with a record date on the following business day, not the same day ([interactive investor](https://www.ii.co.uk/analysis-commentary/ftse-100-ex-dividend-dates-august-2026-ii539870)). That's set to change on a specific, already-legislated date: the UK moves to T+1 on October 11, 2027 — the same date the EU has adopted, via a rule change published in the EU's Official Journal in October 2025, after ESMA recommended it ([Global Relay](https://www.grip.globalrelay.com/uk-will-move-to-t1-cycle-for-securities-settlement-from-october-2027/); [ESMA](https://www.esma.europa.eu/press-news/esma-news/esma-proposes-move-t1-october-2027)). So the UK and most of the EU are currently the odd ones out among major markets, and that's specifically because of where they are in this transition, not a permanent structural difference — worth checking again once that date actually arrives, since settlement cycles have moved faster than expected before and this whole area is worth re-verifying rather than treating as settled.

## One more thing worth connecting

If you've read about [what a bond actually is](/blog/bond), the idea of yield there — what you actually earn relative to the price you paid, not the number printed on the security — has a direct dividend equivalent. Dividend yield is just the annual dividend divided by the share price: Apple's $1.08 a year divided by its current stock price gives you that stock's dividend yield, and it moves every time the price does, exactly the way a bond's yield moves opposite its price. Same underlying idea, different asset.
