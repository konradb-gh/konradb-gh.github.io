As of August 5, 2026's close, WTI crude is up 12.2% since February 27, 2026 — the last trading day before the US and Israel struck Iran. The S&P 500 is up 12.3% over the same stretch. Two numbers that close together, on two assets that are supposed to move in opposite directions during an oil-shock war, looks like proof the market shrugged the whole thing off.

It isn't. Those two nearly-identical numbers are the endpoints of two completely different rides, and the only way to see that is to stop treating "since the war started" as one period.

Quick note before the breakdown: I'm using the S&P 500 here, not the Nasdaq or a narrower benchmark. Its breadth and its market-cap weighting toward large, stable, profitable companies make it the closest single proxy for the health of the broader US economy, rather than for tech sentiment or a handful of overweighted names. That's the actual question this piece is asking, so that's the index that answers it.

## What actually happened, phase by phase

This has not been one continuous war with one continuous market reaction. It's been three distinct regimes, and conflating them is how you end up with a lazy "geopolitical risk is priced in" take that doesn't survive contact with the daily data.

**Phase 1 — active conflict, Feb 28 to Apr 7.** The US and Israel launched Operation Epic Fury on February 28, killing Iran's Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei in the opening strikes. Iran's response was to shut the Strait of Hormuz — the IRGC issued blanket warnings against vessel passage within hours, struck the tanker *Skylight* on March 1, and claimed full control of the waterway by March 4 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Strait_of_Hormuz_crisis)). That's not a symbolic threat. Roughly 20 million barrels a day of crude oil and petroleum products — about 27% of global maritime oil trade and roughly a fifth of world petroleum liquids consumption — normally moves through that strait ([CRS Report R45281](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R45281/R45281.6.pdf)). WTI ran from $67.02 to a period high of $112.95 on April 7, up 68.5%, as the market priced a real, physical supply disruption on a chokepoint that had never actually been closed before. The S&P 500 fell from 6,878.88 to a low of 6,343.72 on March 30, down 7.8%, on the combination of a decapitation strike against a state actor, a spiking 30-year Treasury yield (reported as high as 5.2%), and a CPI print near 4.2% — more than double the Fed's target ([The Hill](https://thehill.com/business/6000693-fed-holds-rates-iran-war/)).

**Phase 2 — the ceasefire lull, Apr 8 to May 6.** Pakistan brokered a ceasefire on April 8. Oil and equities both took it as a signal, but not a clean one — the US Navy started its own blockade of Iranian ports on April 13, even while the ceasefire technically held, producing what observers called a "dual blockade" that kept the strait functionally shut regardless of what the diplomats had agreed to ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Strait_of_Hormuz_crisis)). By April 21 the IMO was reporting roughly 20,000 mariners and 2,000 ships stranded in the Gulf. Oil chopped sideways in a wide band through this stretch instead of trending; equities began grinding higher.

**Phase 3 — the second escalation, May 7 to Aug 5.** On May 7, Iranian forces hit three US warships — the *Truxtun*, *Rafael Peralta*, and *Mason* — with missiles, drones, and small boats in the strait. The US struck back at Iranian military facilities in Bandar Abbas and Qeshm Island the same day ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7_May_2026_United_States_strikes_on_Iran)). Oil spiked again, to a local high of $108.66 (index 162) on May 18. And then, oddly, everything decoupled. The S&P 500 closed at 7,473.45 on May 22, a level the financial press flatly described as a full recovery from the war ([Seeking Alpha](https://seekingalpha.com/article/4908753-sp500-fully-recovers-iran-war-impact-disappears-stock-prices)). Oil kept doing its own thing — it round-tripped almost back to pre-war levels by July 6 (a trend CNBC covered directly, "oil prices return to pre-war levels," on June 29 ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/29/strait-of-hormuz-shipping-iran-oil-war-us.html))), then the truce collapsed on July 8 when Iran hit commercial vessels again, and by August 2 only two ships transited the strait against a normal daily rate of about 73 ([Strait of Hormuz Live Tracker](https://straits.live/)). WTI closed July 31 at $84.67 — matching the print reported by CNBC that day almost to the cent ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/31/oil-prices-today-brent-wti-hormuz-trump-iran-.html)) — before easing back to $75.22 by August 5. The S&P, meanwhile, hit a fresh high of 7,736.52 on August 4 and hasn't looked back.

## The correlation, actually computed

Here's the part that usually gets skipped: an assertion that oil and stocks are "moving opposite" or "decoupled" without anyone running the number. I computed the Pearson correlation between daily percentage returns — not price levels, which would just pick up the fact that both series trend and produce a spuriously clean-looking number — for WTI and the S&P 500 across the full period and within each phase.

- **Full period (Mar 2–Aug 5, n=109 trading days): r = −0.50.** A moderate, real, negative relationship. On an average day, when oil jumps, stocks tend to fall, and vice versa — the textbook oil-shock-as-risk-off pattern, present but far from absolute.
- **Phase 1 — active conflict (n=26): r = −0.48.** Close to the full-period number.
- **Phase 2 — ceasefire lull (n=21): r = −0.80.** The tightest coupling of the three phases, by a wide margin.
- **Phase 3 — second escalation (n=62): r = −0.37.** The weakest.

That ordering surprised me, and it's worth sitting with rather than smoothing over. The correlation wasn't strongest during the shooting war. It was strongest during the ceasefire — the quietest, least violent stretch of the whole period. And it was weakest during the second round of actual combat.

I'd put a caveat on Phase 2 specifically: 21 trading days is a small sample, and a single correlation estimate off that few observations swings around more than people give it credit for. I'm not going to pretend −0.80 is a stable structural constant. But the direction of the finding — tighter in the lull, looser in the second war phase — shows up clearly enough, and for a reason that makes sense once you think about what each period actually was.

## Why the coupling comes and goes

During the ceasefire, there was functionally one story in the market: is this truce real, or is it about to break? Every headline — the dual blockade, the stranded ships, the Islamabad talks — fed into that single question, and it moved oil and equities off the same information in opposite directions, because a "ceasefire holding" headline is bullish for stocks and bearish for oil (less supply risk) at the same time, every time. One dominant narrative, one tight (negative) correlation. That's not really a story about oil driving equities so much as both assets briefly trading as a single referendum on the same fact.

Phase 1 and Phase 3 both had more than one story running, which is exactly why the correlation loosens in both. In Phase 1, oil was purely a supply story — Hormuz physically closed, tankers not moving, 20 million barrels a day of real throughput at risk. Equities were reacting to that and to the assassination of a head of state and to a jump in long-end yields and inflation expectations that had nothing to do with tanker traffic. Two different shocks landing at once dilutes the correlation between any two assets even when both are moving hard.

Phase 3 is the more important case, because the war was still in that phase as of the August 5, 2026 data cutoff. The naval mechanics stayed genuinely disruptive — the dual blockade, the June 20 re-closure, the July 8 attack on commercial vessels, the two-ships-a-day count on August 2 — and oil tracked that reasonably faithfully, spiking on closures and easing on reopening signals. Equities mostly stopped caring. The clearest explanation I've found for why comes from the liquidity side, not the war: with U.S. dollar-denominated debt equal to roughly 64% of global debt, the dollar index rose only about 1.8% over the entire war — nowhere near enough to trigger the kind of liquidity squeeze that actually crashes risk assets — and Treasury swap lines to regional allies kept dollar-denominated oil settlement from seizing up ([io-fund](https://io-fund.com/broad-market/sp-500-shrugs-off-iran-war-liquidity-signal)). Layer the Fed on top: its July 29 meeting held rates steady on a 9-3 vote, with all three dissents wanting a hike rather than a cut, and futures were pricing roughly 82% odds of an actual increase in September, as of July 29 ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/29/fed-rate-decision-july-2026.html)). A market that's spent three months trading the Fed's next move and the durability of a liquidity-fueled, V-shaped-recovery mentality — the Nasdaq-100 erased a 12% correction in 11 days, finishing by April 15, the fastest recovery-to-drawdown ratio on record and the kind of muscle memory that makes investors buy every dip on reflex ([io-fund](https://io-fund.com/broad-market/sp-500-shrugs-off-iran-war-liquidity-signal)) — simply has a stronger, more persistent driver of its own than a war that, from a pure balance-sheet standpoint, keeps not touching the two things (dollar liquidity, Fed policy) that actually move index-level equity prices.

Oil doesn't get that luxury. It has no equivalent independent variable powerful enough to override "is the world's most important chokepoint open or closed this week." That's the whole difference, in one sentence: equities have other places to look; oil, for this specific conflict, structurally does not.

## What this says about how risk is being priced

Here's my actual position, not a hedge: equity markets are not pricing this war as a systemic risk anymore, and on the numbers, they're not entirely wrong to have stopped.

The bear case for staying scared is obvious and I don't want to wave it away — the strait is still not reliably open, two ships a day against a normal rate of 73 is not a resolved crisis, and a fresh escalation that actually disrupted the broader region's oil infrastructure rather than just the strait could still produce a supply shock nothing in the market structure, as of August 5, 2026, is priced for. That risk is real and it's sitting there, un-priced, which is precisely what makes it dangerous — markets that have stopped flinching are the ones that get hit hardest when something finally lands.

But the data I've actually computed here doesn't support "the market is being complacent" as a blanket statement. It supports something narrower and more accurate: equities have correctly identified that this war, through August 5, 2026, has been a contained commodity-market problem rather than a liquidity or growth problem, and they're pricing it that way — not ignoring it, differentiating it. The −0.37 correlation in Phase 3 isn't zero. Oil shocks still knock stocks around on the days they happen; they just don't compound into a trend the way they did in Phase 1, because the balance-sheet-level triggers that turn a regional war into a global equity bear market — dollar scarcity, a hawkish Fed surprise, a credit event — haven't fired. If any one of those three does fire while the strait is still contested, I'd expect the correlation to snap back toward Phase 1's level fast, not stay at Phase 3's. Until then, the market isn't wrong to treat oil and equities as two separate trades. It's just a much narrower bet than "geopolitical risk is priced in" makes it sound, and it's a bet that depends entirely on the Fed and the dollar continuing to behave, not on the war actually ending.
