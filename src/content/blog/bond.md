A bond is a loan you make to a government or a company. In exchange, the borrower promises to pay you back the original amount on a set date, and pay you interest along the way for the use of your money in the meantime.

## The analogy

Say you lend a friend $1,000, in writing: he'll pay you $30 every year for holding your money, and hand back the full $1,000 five years from now, an agreed date. That $30 a year is what he's paying for the use of your cash. The $1,000 he eventually hands back isn't a bonus — it's just your own money coming home.

A bond is that exact arrangement, formalized and put up for trading, usually between an ordinary investor and a government or a company instead of a friend. The $1,000 is the **face value** (or "par value") — what you get back at the end. The $30 a year is the **coupon** — the regular interest payment, fixed at whatever rate was agreed when the bond was issued. And the agreed date you get your $1,000 back is the bond's **maturity**.

## Someone actually doing this

Denise is three years from retiring. She's not trying to grow this chunk of her savings aggressively — she's trying to make sure it's there when she needs it. The stock market could easily be down 20% in three years; it's happened before, for less reason than a global event. So she buys a bond (in practice, more often a bond fund holding many bonds) maturing right around when she plans to stop working. She now knows two things that matter more to her than a shot at a bigger return: roughly what she'll earn, and exactly when she'll have the cash. Giving up upside for certainty on a date is the entire reason bonds exist as something people actually buy, not a textbook asset class.

## Why the price still moves

If Denise holds to maturity, none of this matters — she gets her fixed coupon every year and her $1,000 back at the end, full stop. But bonds also trade before they mature, which is where people get confused, since the coupon and face value never change. What moves is the price someone else will pay for it today.

Say a $1,000 bond pays a 4% coupon — $40 a year — issued back when prevailing rates were around 4%. Now rates in the broader market rise to 6%. Brand-new $1,000 bonds pay $60 a year. Nobody's paying full price for an old bond that only pays $40 when a new one pays $60 for the same money — so the old bond's price has to fall, to somewhere around $650–700, before that fixed $40 works out to roughly the same 6% a new buyer could get elsewhere. That real, current return — what you actually earn given the price you paid, not the rate printed on the bond — is its **yield**. Rates falling does the reverse: the old 4% bond suddenly looks generous, so its price gets bid up instead.

## Government bonds vs. corporate bonds

Both work exactly the way described above. What differs is who's borrowing.

A government bond — a US Treasury note, for instance — funds the government's own spending and deficits (the US federal government is currently financing debt north of $40 trillion this way). A corporate bond funds something the company is doing with the money: expanding, buying another company, or running operations — the recent wave of tech companies borrowing hundreds of billions to build AI infrastructure, which I wrote about in the [Treasury buyback post](/blog/treasury-bond-buyback-2026), is corporate bond issuance at a scale most people never see.

A corporate bond typically pays a higher coupon than a government bond of the same maturity because of credit risk: a company can fail and default on you. A government — especially one, like the US, borrowing in its own currency, which it can always print more of — is considered far less likely to. That extra risk has to be compensated, so a riskier borrower offers a bigger coupon to attract the same money a safer one gets more cheaply.

For Denise, this isn't abstract. The money she genuinely cannot afford to lose — what she's counting on being there, in full, on a specific date — belongs closer to a government bond, where safety is the point and a smaller coupon is the price of it. A separate portion, one she could tolerate seeing shrink, is where a corporate bond's extra yield might actually be worth the risk. Same tool, two different jobs, depending on what that money is actually for.

## Why this matters right now

I wrote earlier about the Fed's [rate hold at 3.50%–3.75%](/blog/fed-september-2026-rate-decision) and about [the Treasury's scramble as 30-year yields hit a 19-year high](/blog/treasury-bond-buyback-2026). That bond's price sliding as its yield spiked is the exact mechanic above, running at a scale of trillions instead of one $1,000 note. And once you understand why bond prices move against rates, the appeal of an [interest rate swap](/blog/interest-rate-swap) makes more sense too — it exists for people who'd rather not be exposed to that swing at all.

None of this tells you which bond to buy, or whether now's a good time to buy one. It just explains why the number on the screen moves even though the $1,000 you're actually owed back never does.
