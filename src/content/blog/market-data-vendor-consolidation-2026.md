A market data vendor is any company that sells data, analytics, or infrastructure to financial firms rather than trading or managing money itself. That distinction matters more than it sounds like it should: a bank, an asset manager, and a hedge fund are all *customers* in this world, not vendors — they pay for the data and the tools, they don't sell them. The vendors are the ones behind the scenes: the company whose price feed populates a trading screen, whose index a fund tracks, whose credit rating gets baked into a covenant, whose spreadsheet plugin pulls a company's financials into a model. It's a business built entirely on being infrastructure other people's businesses run on top of — which is exactly why, right now, the biggest and best-funded customers in that ecosystem have started deciding they'd rather own pieces of the infrastructure than keep renting it.

## The landscape, briefly

This site already covered the two best-known consumer-facing terminals — [LSEG Workspace and Bloomberg Terminal](/blog/lseg-vs-bloomberg-2026) — in real depth, so I won't repeat that comparison here. But the vendor category is much wider than those two, and it's worth naming the other kinds of company actually doing this work, because the consolidation story below touches several of them directly.

**Index and benchmark providers** build and license the reference points the rest of the industry prices against — MSCI's global equity indices and S&P Dow Jones Indices' S&P 500 and Dow Jones Industrial Average are the two most consequential examples, underpinning trillions of dollars in indexed and benchmarked assets.

**Ratings agencies acting as data vendors** is its own real category: Moody's now runs two businesses under one parent — Moody's Ratings, the traditional credit-rating business, and a second, increasingly important arm (long known as Moody's Analytics) that sells company reference data, risk models, and compliance software separately from the ratings themselves.

**Research and analytics platforms** like FactSet sell the workstation-and-data-feed combination a research analyst or portfolio manager actually works inside all day, serving several thousand client organizations worldwide.

**Private-markets specialists** are a newer, narrower category: firms built specifically to cover the part of the market that doesn't trade on an exchange and doesn't file public disclosures on a predictable schedule — fund performance, private company valuations, deal terms. Preqin, before the acquisition that's the center of this post, was one of the best-known independent names in exactly that space.

Every one of those categories sells something a bank, asset manager, or corporate treasury team would otherwise have to build itself. And building it yourself, it turns out, is exactly what some of the largest customers have started doing.

## BlackRock buying Preqin: a client absorbing a vendor

BlackRock — the world's largest asset manager, not a data company — agreed to acquire Preqin, a leading independent private-markets data provider, for £2.55 billion (roughly $3.2 billion) in an all-cash deal announced June 30, 2024. The deal closed March 3, 2025, after regulatory clearance ([BlackRock](https://www.blackrock.com/corporate/newsroom/media/press-releases/blackrock-completes-preqin-acquisition); [Financier Worldwide](https://www.financierworldwide.com/fw-news/2024/7/2/blackrock-to-take-preqin-private-in-32bn-deal)).

Preqin wasn't a small, speculative buy. It came with global coverage of roughly 190,000 funds, 60,000 fund managers, and 30,000 private-markets investors, plus more than 4,000 institutional client relationships and about $240 million of highly recurring 2024 revenue ([BlackRock](https://www.blackrock.com/corporate/newsroom/media/press-releases/blackrock-completes-preqin-acquisition)). That's a real, established business with its own paying customer base — some of whom, notably, are BlackRock's own competitors, who now have to decide whether they're comfortable buying private-markets data from a company owned by one of the largest asset managers on earth.

BlackRock's own stated rationale was explicit: combine Preqin's private-markets data and research tools with the workflow capabilities of Aladdin — BlackRock's own investment and risk-management operating system, used by institutional investors including banks, pensions, corporations, and insurers globally — into what it called "a preeminent private markets technology and data provider" spanning public and private markets in one platform ([BlackRock](https://www.blackrock.com/corporate/newsroom/media/press-releases/blackrock-completes-preqin-acquisition)).

This isn't a new instinct for BlackRock. Preqin is a repeat of a playbook the firm already ran once before: in 2019, BlackRock acquired eFront, described at the time as the industry-leading alternatives investment management software provider, for $1.3 billion in cash, buying it from private equity firm Bridgepoint and eFront's own employees. The deal was agreed March 22, 2019 and completed May 10, 2019. eFront covered the operational side of alternatives investing — due diligence, portfolio planning, performance and risk analysis — for more than 700 clients across 48 countries, and BlackRock folded it into Aladdin the same way it's now folding in Preqin ([BlackRock](https://www.blackrock.com/corporate/newsroom/media/press-releases/alternatives-investment-software-provider); [GlobeSt](https://www.globest.com/2019/03/25/blackrock-to-buy-efront-for-1-3b/)).

Put the two together and the pattern is unmistakable: a single client, six years apart, buying two different pieces of the alternatives-data stack — one for operational workflow (eFront), one for the underlying data itself (Preqin) — and bringing both inside its own platform rather than continuing to license them from independent vendors indefinitely. That's vertical integration in its purest form: the customer becomes its own supplier.

## MSCI's acquisition spree: a vendor consolidating vendors

MSCI's recent moves are the mirror image. MSCI isn't a client absorbing a vendor's capabilities — it's already a major vendor in its own right, and it's been buying smaller, more specialized vendors at a genuinely rapid pace over the last twelve months.

**Vantager**, acquired March 2, 2026: an AI-native platform, founded in 2024 and based in New York, built to automate pre-investment due diligence for private-markets investors — specifically, extracting and structuring the unstructured documents general partners hand institutional investors during fundraising, so limited partners can move through diligence faster and with more consistency ([MSCI](https://www.msci.com/discover-msci/media-room/msci-transforms-private-markets-pre-investment-diligence-with-acquisition-of-vantager)).

**Compass Financial Technologies**, acquired one day later, March 3, 2026: a Swiss index-services provider MSCI bought specifically to expand its own ability to create, calculate, and customize complex multi-asset indexes spanning equities, fixed income, commodities, digital assets, currencies, and derivatives — building directly on MSCI's earlier 2024 acquisition of Foxberry, another index-services specialist ([MSCI](https://www.msci.com/discover-msci/media-room/msci-expands-multi-asset-and-alternative-index-capabilities-with-acquisition-of-compass-financial-technologies)).

**PM Insights**, acquired roughly a month later, announced April 7, 2026: a specialist private-markets data firm with offices in New York, London, and Singapore, providing daily secondary-market reference data — pricing, valuation, transaction activity, and liquidity — for private company securities, aggregated from a network of institutional broker-dealers and bank secondary desks. MSCI said the dataset covers private companies representing an estimated $5.5 trillion in market cap as of the acquisition date, and framed the deal around improving pricing transparency in private equity as companies stay private for longer ([MSCI](https://www.msci.com/discover-msci/media-room/msci-advances-transparency-in-private-markets-with-acquisition-of-pm-insights)). Terms weren't disclosed for either Compass or PM Insights, and MSCI said neither deal is expected to be financially material on its own — which tells you something too: these aren't bet-the-company acquisitions, they're a steady, deliberate accumulation of specialist capability, one narrow piece at a time.

Three acquisitions in roughly thirteen months, each one a small, specific, previously-independent data or analytics business getting absorbed into MSCI's own product line rather than staying a competitor or a potential partner. That's horizontal consolidation: a vendor buying other vendors to widen what it can sell under its own name, rather than a customer buying a vendor to stop having to pay one.

## Why both patterns are happening at once

Neither of these is happening in a vacuum, and neither is just "M&A is normal, companies buy each other sometimes." Three real forces are pushing in the same direction.

The first is the sheer size of what's being priced. According to McKinsey's Global Private Markets Report, traditional closed-end private markets AUM reached roughly $16–16.5 trillion by 2025, growing 5–10% year over year, while the broader universe of alternative capital grew even faster, up 10–15% to around $8–8.5 trillion ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/private-capital/our-insights/global-private-markets-report)). Public-market data has decades of standardized infrastructure behind it — exchanges, regulators, disclosure requirements. Private markets have none of that by default: no exchange tape, no mandatory quarterly filings, no single source of truth on what a private company is actually worth this week. As the asset class priced in that gap has roughly doubled in scale over a decade, the data layer covering it has gone from a nice-to-have to a genuine competitive necessity — which is exactly why PM Insights, Preqin, and Vantager all exist as businesses in the first place, and exactly why owning one now looks worth billions.

The second is the shift toward AI-ready structured data specifically. The generic version of this claim — "everyone wants AI now" — isn't interesting on its own, but the specific mechanism is: an AI model is only as useful as the structured, governed data you feed it, and a large share of the financial industry's most valuable information (GP due-diligence documents, private valuation marks, bespoke index methodologies) has historically existed as unstructured PDFs and one-off spreadsheets, not clean, queryable data. Vantager's entire business is turning unstructured GP documents into structured output; Compass's is letting MSCI programmatically construct indexes across asset classes that don't naturally share a data format. Buying that capability outright is faster than building it internally from scratch, and it's happening specifically because the payoff for having AI-ready data now is larger than it was even two or three years ago.

The third is scale economics, and this one is closer to home for anyone who's worked inside a data organization: at a certain size, the math flips. Licensing a vendor's data forever means paying that vendor's margin forever, on top of whatever it actually costs to produce the data. Once a firm is large enough — and BlackRock managing trillions in assets, or MSCI already running one of the industry's largest index businesses, both clear that bar comfortably — owning the pipeline outright, even at a $1.3–3.2 billion price tag, can be cheaper over a long enough horizon than an indefinite licensing relationship, especially once you also capture the ability to sell that data or capability onward to your own clients instead of just consuming it.

## Not a new story — just a faster one

This site has already covered one earlier chapter of exactly this pattern: LSEG's $27 billion acquisition of Refinitiv, announced in 2019 and completed January 29, 2021, folding one of the industry's largest data-and-analytics businesses (built on Reuters' decades of market infrastructure) into what's now LSEG Workspace — [covered in full elsewhere on this site](/blog/lseg-vs-bloomberg-2026). LSEG itself wasn't a data vendor going into that deal — it was, first and foremost, an exchange and clearing operator, which on the surface looks closer to BlackRock's position than MSCI's. What puts it on the MSCI side of the line is what the deal actually built: LSEG Data & Analytics is now a real, externally-sold business in its own right, serving more than 40,000 customer organizations and roughly 400,000 end users worldwide, not a data capability LSEG folded in purely to run its own exchange more efficiently. That's the meaningful distinction from BlackRock's Aladdin-focused integration — the outcome was a vendor selling data to the rest of the industry, not a client internalizing data for itself. So the deal is itself a vendor-consolidates-vendor story, the same pattern MSCI is now running at a smaller, faster, more frequent scale. What's changed isn't the existence of this pattern — it's the pace. LSEG's Refinitiv deal was a single, once-a-decade, industry-reshaping move. MSCI has now run three comparable (if individually much smaller) deals inside about thirteen months. Consolidation in this industry isn't a new phenomenon. It's just moving noticeably faster than it used to.

## What this actually means going forward

Here's the real position, not a hedge: this consolidation is quietly concentrating the best data in fewer hands, and it's happening at both ends of the market at once, which is the part that should actually worry a smaller player.

If you're a firm the size of BlackRock, this trend is good news twice over — you get to buy the specialist capability you need at a price that's rational relative to your own scale, and you stop paying an outside vendor's margin on data that's now core to your own product. If you're MSCI, it's good news for a similar reason from the other side: each acquisition widens what you can sell under one roof, and the price tags involved (mostly undisclosed, explicitly described as immaterial to MSCI's own financials) tell you these are affordable, incremental bets for a company MSCI's size, not stretch acquisitions.

If you're a mid-sized asset manager, a smaller hedge fund, or a corporate treasury team that isn't BlackRock-scale, neither pattern is good news for you specifically. You can't run BlackRock's playbook — buying an entire specialist data provider outright isn't a realistic option at your size — and you're increasingly buying access to data that used to come from several independent, competing vendors and now increasingly comes from a shrinking handful of much larger ones, each with less competitive pressure to keep prices down or stay maximally open to firms outside their own client ecosystem. That's the uncomfortable part of this trend that a purely celebratory "consolidation drives efficiency" framing tends to skip: fewer independent vendors generally means less competitive pricing pressure over time, even when each individual deal makes obvious sense for the two companies involved.

None of this is a prediction about where any specific stock goes. It's a read on where pricing power in financial data is actually heading — toward the handful of firms large enough to either build their own data in-house or buy enough specialists to stop needing anyone else's, and away from everyone still renting access one license at a time.
