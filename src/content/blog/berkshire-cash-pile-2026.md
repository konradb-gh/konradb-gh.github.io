Two figures for Berkshire Hathaway's cash pile at the end of the first quarter of 2026 are floating around in press coverage right now: $397.4 billion and $497.4 billion. Only one of them is real. I went to the actual 10-Q Berkshire filed with the SEC rather than trust either secondary number, and the balance sheet is unambiguous: $51,478 million in cash and cash equivalents plus $339,261 million in short-term Treasury Bills, held by the insurance and other businesses, plus another $6,644 million in cash sitting in the railroad, utilities and energy segment. Add it up and you get $397,383 million — $397.4 billion, to the dollar. The $497.4 billion figure isn't supported by anything in the filing; it looks like a transcription error that made it into circulation and got repeated. By June 30, 2026, the same math — $35,096 million cash plus $324,905 million in Treasury Bills in the insurance business, plus $5,513 million at the railroad, utilities and energy segment — gives $365,514 million, or $365.5 billion. That's the real range: a genuine, record $397.4 billion peak, drawn down to $365.5 billion three months later.

That $32 billion isn't a rounding change. It's the first real move on a pile that took years to build, and it happened almost entirely on Greg Abel's watch, not Warren Buffett's. Buffett stepped down as CEO at the end of 2025; Abel became CEO on January 1, 2026. Getting that sequencing right matters for how you read everything that follows — this isn't Buffett quietly reversing his own decade of caution. It's the person who succeeded him making a different call with the pile he inherited.

## How the pile got that big in the first place

The buildup itself was unambiguously a Buffett-era decision, and it was a long one. Berkshire's cash and Treasury Bill position — insurance-and-other plus the railroad/utilities/energy segment, the same combined figure reported above — closed out five straight year-ends like this, each figure pulled from that year's own originally filed 10-K:

![Berkshire's cash and Treasury Bill pile, year-end 2021 through Q2 2026](/charts/brk-cash-pile-2026.png)

*Source: Berkshire Hathaway 10-K and 10-Q filings, SEC EDGAR. Insurance-and-other cash and short-term U.S. Treasury Bills, plus railroad/utilities/energy segment cash, each figure from that period's own originally filed report.*

$146.7 billion at the end of 2021. It actually shrank to $128.6 billion in 2022 — Berkshire was still buying that year. Then it climbed to $167.6 billion in 2023, roughly doubled again to $334.2 billion in 2024, and kept climbing to $373.3 billion by the end of 2025, before topping out at that $397.4 billion peak in the first quarter of 2026. The mechanism behind the 2024-2025 acceleration is well documented: Buffett sold a large majority of Berkshire's Apple stake over that stretch, and the proceeds went into Treasury Bills instead of new equity purchases. Berkshire went fourteen consecutive quarters as a net seller of stocks — selling more than it bought every single quarter for three and a half straight years, starting in late 2022 — before Q2 2026 finally broke the streak: $23.47 billion bought against $3.69 billion sold, a net purchase of roughly $19.8 billion in a single quarter.

Buffett was never quiet about why. In his final letter as CEO, covering fiscal 2024, he put it about as plainly as an 8-word sentence can: "We can't come and go on a dime." Berkshire's scale is the whole problem in that sentence — a company with a market capitalization north of a trillion dollars can't meaningfully move its own needle with a $500 million investment, and a $50 billion one is disqualifying for all but a handful of companies on earth. That shrinks the usable universe of deals regardless of how the broader market is priced. Layered on top of that constraint is discipline about price, which Buffett described the same way he always has: most of the time, nothing clears the bar. "Often, nothing looks compelling; very infrequently we find ourselves knee-deep in opportunities," he wrote in that same letter. The cash wasn't sitting there because Buffett had lost conviction in stocks. It was sitting there because the two filters — big enough to matter, cheap enough to be worth it — were both failing at once, for years.

## What actually changed in 2026

What Abel did with the pile in the first half of 2026 is concrete enough to walk through line by line, all verified against Berkshire's own filings rather than secondhand summaries.

Berkshire bought $39.4 billion of equity securities in the first six months of 2026 (versus $27.8 billion sold), a sharp reversal from the same period the year before. The single largest piece of that was a $10 billion private placement into Alphabet, agreed on June 1 as part of Alphabet's broader $80 billion capital raise for AI infrastructure — $5 billion of Class A shares at $351.81 and $5 billion of Class C shares at $348.20, priced and negotiated directly rather than bought up in the open market, which is exactly the kind of access that avoids moving the price against yourself when you're buying $10 billion of anything. Separately, Berkshire closed the $6.8 billion all-cash acquisition of Taylor Morrison, a national homebuilder, on July 24 — a deal that had been under a signed merger agreement since May 31 and cleared a shareholder vote on July 22, not a snap decision. On top of that, Berkshire spent about $4.5 billion buying back its own stock in the second quarter alone (essentially nothing in April, $0.7 billion in May, $3.8 billion in June, based on Berkshire's own monthly repurchase disclosure), and the pace didn't let up — the reduction in shares outstanding between the June 30 balance sheet date and the July 29 filing-cover-page date implies roughly $3.3 billion more bought back in July.

All of this, worth repeating, is Abel's decision under Berkshire's own governance structure — the buyback authority explicitly sits with "Berkshire's Chief Executive Officer, after consultation with the Chairman of the Board," which is the company's own language for what's now Abel deciding and Buffett being consulted, not the reverse. Buffett's current role, in Abel's own words from his first annual letter as CEO, is Chairman — "in the office five days a week," available to weigh in, not the one making the call anymore. Available for counsel. Not running the trade.

## The "stay invested" tension isn't really a tension

It's tempting to read a $397 billion cash pile next to Buffett's decades of "buy index funds, don't try to time the market" advice to ordinary investors and call it hypocrisy. It isn't, and the distinction is one Buffett drew explicitly, not one I'm inferring after the fact.

The advice to individual investors was always about a different problem than the one Berkshire itself has. An ordinary investor doesn't have $400 billion to place and can't move any stock's price by buying it; for that investor, time in a broad index beats trying to pick moments, because the picking is usually worse than random and the fees compound against you either way. Berkshire's problem is the opposite one: it has so much capital that most opportunities are too small to matter, and the ones large enough to matter are, most of the time, not priced attractively enough to clear Buffett's bar. Those are genuinely different disciplines solving genuinely different constraints, not a rule for retail investors and an exception Buffett grants himself.

And the cash pile was never actually a substitute for equity exposure — it sat alongside one, not instead of one. Even at the point when commentators were calling Berkshire's cash position "extraordinary," Buffett's own answer, in that same fiscal 2024 letter, was blunt: "the great majority of your money remains in equities. That preference won't change." Berkshire's marketable equity portfolio fell from $354 billion to $272 billion over the course of 2024 alone, almost entirely the Apple selling — still hardly nothing — and, in the same letter, he was explicit that the value of Berkshire's wholly-owned, non-quoted businesses (the railroads, the insurers, See's Candies, Precision Castparts, and the rest) "remains far greater than the value of the marketable portfolio." The cash pile was the excess sitting on top of an already massive invested position, not a retreat from one.

## Is this Abel finding real value, or a new CEO wanting to look busy?

Here's my actual read, not a hedge: this looks like genuine, disciplined deployment, not a changing-of-the-guard trying to manufacture activity — and the market's reaction is telling you something different from what you'd expect if it agreed with me.

The shape of the deployment argues against "optics." A private placement negotiated directly with Alphabet at a fixed price, and a homebuilder acquisition that took nearly two months from signed agreement to shareholder vote to close, are not the moves of someone trying to generate a headline before an earnings call. Those take real underwriting. The buyback pace tells the same story from a different angle — it built from essentially nothing in April to $3.8 billion in June to an estimated $3.3 billion-plus in July, which is a company getting more comfortable committing capital over time, not a one-quarter show of force timed to a leadership transition.

And yet Berkshire's stock is up just 0.27% year-to-date through August 14, against the S&P 500's 13.74% — a gap of roughly 13.5 percentage points, computed directly from the closing prices rather than any secondhand estimate, and it hasn't meaningfully closed even after two quarters of real buying and a well-covered earnings report in early August.

I don't think that gap is a verdict on Abel's judgment. I think it's a mismatch between what's driving the S&P 500 in 2026 and what Berkshire actually owns. This year's index return is disproportionately a small number of AI-infrastructure and mega-cap tech names doing an enormous amount of the work — the same dynamic this site has already documented pulling the Nasdaq around on its own terms. Berkshire's book is insurance, railroads, utilities, consumer brands, and a value-tilted equity portfolio anchored in Apple, American Express, Bank of America and Coca-Cola. The $10 billion Alphabet placement is Berkshire's most direct swing at that theme, and it's one name inside a much larger, still fundamentally non-AI-weighted company. Buying good businesses at fair prices doesn't make you a beneficiary of a rally that's concentrated almost entirely in a handful of names you don't own much of. The deployment can be exactly the right capital allocation call and still not show up in the stock, because the stock isn't really being priced on Berkshire's capital allocation this year — it's being priced on how much AI-capex exposure it has, which is not very much.

So: real deployment, the right kind of real, and a market that isn't rewarding it for a reason that has nothing to do with whether it was a good decision. Those can both be true at once, and I think they are.
