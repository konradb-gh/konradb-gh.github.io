Every experiment in this series so far has landed in one of two places: no real effect at all, or a real effect nobody could actually reach or afford to trade. This one — from my own [trading-research repo](https://github.com/konradb-gh/trading-research), [Experiment 07](https://github.com/konradb-gh/trading-research/tree/main/experiments/07-index-reconstitution), specifically — is different, and the difference is the whole reason it's worth a post of its own. It finds a real effect that's precisely located — and the version of it anyone could actually trade doesn't just fail to capture it. It reliably loses money, more often than random chance would. That's a sharper, more useful kind of answer than "there's nothing here," and it's worth understanding exactly why.

Quick refreshers if you're new here, both covered in full in the [Experiment 05](/blog/stop-fill-assumption-backtest-2026) and [Experiment 06](/blog/gap-entries-backtest-2026) posts: trades are measured in **R** (your result as a multiple of what you risked) and **expectancy** (the average result per trade); and a **look-ahead bound** is a deliberately impossible, best-case version of a trade — entering before you could actually have known to — used only to set a ceiling on what's possible, never reported as something you could have captured. Both ideas come back here, this time doing more work than usual.

## A different kind of bet than everything else in this series

Every strategy tested earlier in this project — buying a dip, buying a gap, following momentum — is a bet on *human behavior*: that people tend to panic-sell short-term dips, or overreact to news, or keep chasing what's already been going up. Human behavior is never guaranteed. It's a pattern that has shown up before and might not show up next time.

This experiment tests something structurally different: a bet on a **mechanical cause**, not a psychological one. An index fund — a fund built to hold exactly the companies in an index like the S&P 500, in the same proportions — isn't allowed to have an opinion about whether a newly added company is a good investment. Its whole job is to match the index. So when a company gets added to the S&P 500, every fund tracking it is contractually required to go out and buy it, on a known date, regardless of what anyone actually thinks the stock is worth. That's about as close to forced, price-insensitive buying as public markets ever produce — not a pattern that might repeat, but a mechanical certainty that recurs every time a company joins the index.

## How the test finds where that pressure actually shows up

Nobody knows in advance exactly which day captures the real move from this forced buying — it could build for a week beforehand, or show up mostly on the day itself, or bleed out afterward. So rather than guessing, this experiment buys at many different points in time relative to the **effective date** (the official day a company's index membership actually changes) and compares the results, tracing out where the effect actually lives rather than assuming it. Think of it as testing entry points at day −10, day −5, day −1, the day itself, day +5, and so on, all the way out to plus and minus twelve trading days, to map the whole shape rather than guess at one point on it.

## The deletion side: a quick story about knowing when not to bother

Before running any of that expensive testing, the researchers ran a cheaper check first: given how few of these events there are and how much these particular stocks bounce around day to day, how small an effect could a sample this size even detect? If a test can't reliably tell a real 2% move apart from ordinary noise, running it anyway and getting a "no effect" result doesn't actually tell you the effect is absent — it just means the test wasn't big enough to see clearly, which is a different and much less useful finding.

That check killed the deletion side of the experiment before a single real trade was simulated. Companies that get *removed* from the index often go on to get delisted entirely — many go bankrupt or get bought out — which means a huge share of them have no usable price history left at all in free stock-price data. With only about 57 to 64 usable events per test point, the check found this side of the test could only reliably detect effects somewhere between roughly 3% and 18.5% per trade — far larger than anything a real reconstitution effect is expected to produce. The researchers ran the full test anyway, specifically so the underpowering could be *shown* rather than just asserted, and the result came in exactly where the check said it had to: statistically indistinguishable from buying a random stock on the same days, at the 44th percentile (out of 200 random comparisons — more on what that percentile number means in a moment). That's not a disappointing result. It's the power check working exactly as intended, correctly predicting an inconclusive answer *before* any real effort was spent chasing one — a useful habit that paid for itself here even though it never got to produce a dramatic finding.

## The addition side: the heart of this experiment

This is where the real result lives, and the shape of it is intuitive once you see it laid out.

Picture a popular local restaurant that's about to be featured on a major TV show. Word leaks out a few days before the segment actually airs — a supplier talks, a delivery truck with the film crew's logo gets spotted, whatever it is — and a small crowd starts building early, wait times creeping up. By the night before the episode airs, the place is packed, prices for a table are effectively at their peak. Then the segment actually airs. Everyone who wanted to be there because of the hype has already shown up, and the crowd that was building on *anticipation* has nothing left to anticipate. The rush doesn't get bigger — it fades, sometimes overshooting back down as the excitement-driven visitors move on to whatever's next.

That's almost exactly the shape this experiment measured, day by day, in the real S&P 500 addition data:

The price climbs steadily for about two weeks, accelerates hard in the final few sessions, peaks the day *before* the stock officially joins the index — up **+6.46%** cumulatively by that point — and then gives a chunk of it back once the effective date actually arrives. The single biggest single-day move in the whole window is that same day right before the change takes effect (+1.38% that one day alone, a result so far outside normal daily noise it's essentially certain to be real rather than coincidence) — which lines up exactly with when index funds traditionally execute their required trades. And the reversal starts precisely on the effective date itself, the first clearly negative day in the whole window.

This shape is also doing double duty as a sanity check on the research itself: it's specific and well-documented enough in real academic literature (this exact temporary-price-pressure pattern was first measured in the 1980s) that seeing it appear cleanly in this experiment's own data confirms the dates and calculations are actually lined up correctly, not just something that happened to look plausible.

## Two different questions, two different answers

Here's where the "real effect, unreachable version" theme from earlier experiments both repeats and breaks, in the same result.

**Testing whether you could have gotten in before the public announcement — the impossible, best-case version — confirms the effect is completely real.** Buying at various points before the effective date, the results beat a matched random comparison (buying a random other index-eligible stock on the same days, at the same cost, as the fair baseline) at the *100th percentile* — meaning literally all 200 random comparison trades did worse. That's about as clean a "this isn't chance" result as this kind of test can produce. But it's genuinely unreachable, not merely inconvenient: getting in early enough to capture the strongest part of this move would require knowing a company was about to be added roughly five trading days before S&P's public announcement even happens. That's not a speed problem you could engineer around with a faster system — the information required simply doesn't exist yet for anyone trading on public information.

**Testing the version anyone could actually trade — buying only once the change is public knowledge — doesn't land at "no better than random." It lands reliably worse than random.** Buying on the effective date itself and holding five trading days comes in at the 0.5th percentile against that same random comparison; holding twenty trading days lands at the 0.0th percentile, meaning every single one of the 200 random comparisons did better. The one configuration that manages a positive return — waiting a further ten trading days before buying — still only reaches the 92nd percentile, just short of the 95th-percentile bar this kind of result needs to clear to count as real.

The reason isn't complicated once you see the chart again: by the time the information is public and a real trading system could actually act on it, most of the forced buying that drove the price up has already happened. Buying on the effective date means buying near the top of a run-up that's already finishing, not getting ahead of one. It's the restaurant crowd on the night the segment airs — you're paying peak prices for a rush that's already about to fade, not getting a good table before anyone else knew to show up.

## A pattern this project keeps finding: the edge shrinks as people learn about it

Splitting the data into an earlier half and a later half (an ordinary robustness check, done here without anyone specifically looking for this) turned up something extra: all three of the unreachable "before you could know" measurements shrink substantially in the second half of the data compared to the first. Buying at offset −10 and holding ten trading days goes from worth about 8% in the earliest years to well under 3% in the later years — the most dramatic of the three, a roughly two-thirds decline. Buying at offset −6 and holding five trading days almost exactly halves. Buying at offset −4 and holding three trading days shrinks the least, by about two-fifths. A smaller version of the same pattern shows up at every other point tested too. Meanwhile, the negative result on the reachable side gets *worse*, not better, in the more recent half — if anything in this data is strengthening over time, it's the reversal, not the run-up.

This matches real, published history on this exact phenomenon. An effect this mechanically simple and this well-documented attracts enough attention over time — more funds and traders positioning ahead of known index changes — that part of its own edge gets competed away, the same story this research series has now run into more than once. The unreachable side shrinking over time doesn't undermine the finding; if anything, watching an already-documented decay show up on its own, in a totally separate dataset, without anyone looking for it, is exactly the kind of confirmation that makes a result more trustworthy, not less.

## A brief, useful connection to the last post in this series

While double-checking this experiment's trade data for errors, the researchers found a corrupted price record: a stock's price jumping from about $12 to $22,900 between two consecutive trading days with no gap at all in the calendar — obviously not a real trade, since the ticker had quietly had a completely different, unrelated stock spliced onto the same symbol. Chasing that error down turned out to matter well beyond this one experiment: it's the exact same underlying data flaw that had caused the mistaken figures in this project's [Experiment 06 post](/blog/gap-entries-backtest-2026), corrected there right before this experiment was published. This audit is, literally, where that earlier fix came from — one genuinely useful piece of continuity for anyone who's been following the series, not a detour.

## Checking the real trading system's own history

One more honest check: did this exact pattern ever accidentally show up in the live trading system's own years of history, without anyone having built a rule around it? Cross-referencing the system's actual historical buy signals against real addition dates, out of nearly 10,800 signals total, only 42 happened to land within the window this experiment flags as the losing zone — roughly two and a half times a year. Those 42 did underperform, in the same direction this experiment's finding would predict, but the sample is so small that the result isn't statistically distinguishable from noise. Removing those 42 signals entirely would have nudged the whole system's overall performance by an amount roughly 16 times smaller than the normal, ordinary uncertainty already built into that performance number — in other words, too small to even measure against the system's own natural wobble, let alone act on.

That's not a disappointing footnote — it's a legitimate, useful answer to a fair question. "This effect is real, but too rare to matter for how this particular system actually trades" is a complete, honest conclusion in its own right, not a consolation prize for not finding a rule worth adding.

## The verdict

Every earlier experiment in this series mostly answered one question: is there an edge here worth trying to capture? Mostly, the answer was no, or not practically. This experiment answers a sharper and more useful question for anyone tempted to trade this specific pattern: should you actively avoid doing this? And the honest answer is yes.

Not "there's nothing here" — there plainly is something here, confirmed about as cleanly as this kind of test confirms anything. But trying to trade the part of it you can actually see doesn't just fail to capture the effect. It costs you money, more often than a coin flip would, because by the time you can legally and practically act on public information, the price has already done almost everything it was going to do, and what's left is the fade back down. That's a meaningfully different, more actionable finding than "no edge" — it's not a strategy to skip, it's a specific mistake worth ruling out on purpose.

This was not added to the live trading system, for the least surprising reason possible: there's no version of "buy stocks right as they join the index" that this data supports, and there's a version this data actively argues against. The negative result here is the useful output, not an unremarkable dead end — a real, precisely measured thing to actively avoid is worth more than a strategy that never existed in the first place.
