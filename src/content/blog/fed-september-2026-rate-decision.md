At the July 29 meeting, the FOMC held the federal funds rate steady at 3.50%–3.75%. Nothing unusual there — except how it held. The vote was 9-3, and all three dissenters wanted to move in the opposite direction from what most people expect a dissent to mean: Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari, and Dallas's Lorie Logan all preferred a quarter-point hike. That's a hawkish dissent, not a dovish one — three regional presidents on record saying rates aren't high enough yet. By late July, that dissent — plus a May CPI print at 4.2% year-over-year, the highest in three years — had CME's FedWatch tool pricing the odds of a September hike near 80% ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/29/fed-rate-decision-july-2026.html)).

Then, on August 7, the July jobs report landed, and the hike case took a direct hit. Nonfarm payrolls fell by 23,000, against a forecast of an 83,000 gain — an outright contraction, not just a soft beat-miss. Prior months were revised down by a combined 146,000, putting the total shortfall against expectations at roughly 252,000 jobs. The unemployment rate actually ticked down to 4.1%, but for the wrong reason: mostly a further drop in the number of people working or looking for work, not real hiring strength. Wage growth slowed too — average hourly earnings rose 3.2% year-over-year, the slowest pace since May 2021 ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/07/jobs-report-july-2026.html)).

The odds moved fast. CME's FedWatch had the probability of a September hold at roughly one-in-three a week earlier, 45% by the Thursday before the report, and around 60% within hours of the release on August 7. Kalshi, the prediction market, showed roughly 65% odds of a hold the same day ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/07/odds-the-fed-hikes-in-september-tumble-following-big-july-jobs-miss.html)). A meeting that was shaping up as the Fed's first live hike under new leadership is now, on the numbers, more likely to end in no change at all.

The rest of the data released since the July meeting points the same direction. June's CPI, released July 14, cooled to 3.5% year-over-year — the first pullback in the annual rate since January — with core inflation (excluding food and energy) at 2.6% ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/14/consumer-price-index-inflation-report-june-2026.html)). Some of that cooling was energy prices falling as tensions around the Iran war eased that month. Second-quarter GDP, released July 30 as an advance estimate, grew at just 1.5% annualized, down from 2.1% in the first quarter and below consensus, dragged by weaker government spending, investment, and exports — a real deceleration from the "expanding at a solid pace" language the Fed used in its own July 29 statement, a description that was already sitting on top of data that hadn't been published yet. One genuine bright spot in that report: real final sales to private domestic purchasers — GDP stripped of trade, inventories, and government spending — still grew a robust 3.9% annualized, which is the strongest single argument left for the hike camp ([BEA](https://www.bea.gov/news/2026/gdp-advance-estimate-2nd-quarter-2026)).

Kevin Warsh, who took the chair on May 22 after one of the most contentious confirmation fights in Fed history — a 54-45 Senate vote, the closest in the modern era — has been open about wanting to give markets fewer signals than his predecessors did. He kept the dot plot alive as a tool at his first meeting running it, in June 2026, but declined to submit his own dot, saying individual rate projections weren't helpful to the "current policy conjuncture" ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/policy/articles/no-dot-plot-no-forward-113128646.html)). He's also signaled a preference for leaning on the Fed's balance sheet over rate moves as a policy lever. A chair who telegraphs less, combined with two straight economic reports landing softer than expected, is exactly the setup that produces more uncertainty into a meeting, not less.

It isn't a settled call, though, and I don't want to overstate how settled it is. Fed Governor Lisa Cook has said she'd still support a hike "if it becomes necessary to bring inflation down," and Morgan Stanley's Ellen Zentner has made the case that a cooler labor market alone might not be enough to quiet the hike argument inside the committee if the next inflation print comes in hot. That next print is July's CPI, due August 12 — five weeks before the meeting, and the single data point most likely to decide this either way. Capital Economics' Thomas Ryan put it plainly: it would take a real upside surprise there to keep a September hike alive ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/policy/article/weak-july-jobs-report-helps-case-for-fed-to-hold-rates-but-doesnt-take-hikes-off-the-table-134001140.html)).

Here's my actual position: I think the Fed holds in September. A genuine payroll contraction plus 146,000 jobs' worth of downward revisions is a harder thing for a data-dependent committee to explain away than one hot CPI print was — and unlike on July 29, the growth side of the argument has cracked too, with GDP at 1.5% instead of the "solid pace" the Fed was describing. The clean case for tightening that existed on July 29 no longer exists in the same form.

I wouldn't call it locked in. Inflation is still running well above the Fed's 2% target, core CPI hasn't fallen as far as headline CPI has, and energy prices are only one Iran-war escalation away from pushing both back up — which is exactly the dynamic that drove May's spike in the first place. The July 29 hawks haven't recanted anything; they were arguing from an inflation problem that's cooled but hasn't disappeared. If the August 12 CPI print surprises to the upside, this piece's conclusion is wrong within days of being published. That's not a hedge — it's the actual shape of the risk here, and it's a shorter distance to a reversal than most of what gets called "data-dependent" actually is.
