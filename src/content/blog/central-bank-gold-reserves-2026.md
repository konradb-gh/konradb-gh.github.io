Gold gained 65% in 2025 — its best year since 1980 — then kept going, touching a record above $5,589 an ounce in late January 2026 ([Gold & Silver](https://goldsilver.com/industry-news/article/gold-price-forecasts-for-2026-revisited-after-q1/)) before giving back a chunk of that spike. That price action is the headline. It's not the interesting part. The interesting part is who's been buying, in what size, and why the answer to that question has quietly rewritten how gold actually gets priced.

## The supply and demand picture

Start with the numbers, because they're more specific than "gold is popular right now." Total gold demand topped 5,000 tonnes for the first time in 2025, matched almost exactly by total supply of 5,002.3 tonnes — mine production hit a record 3,671.6 tonnes, just past the old 2018 high, and recycled gold added another 1,404.3 tonnes ([World Gold Council](https://www.gold.org/goldhub/research/gold-demand-trends/gold-demand-trends-full-year-2025/supply)). That recycling figure is worth pausing on: it rose only 3% despite gold's average price jumping 44% for the year. Normally a price spike that large pulls old jewelry out of drawers and into scrap. It didn't, mostly because buyers in India and the Middle East kept pledging gold as loan collateral instead of selling it outright — a behavioral pattern, not a supply constraint.

On the demand side, four buyer types matter, and they didn't move together. Central banks bought 863 tonnes net in 2025 — down 21% from 2024's 1,092 tonnes, and the lowest annual total since 2021, but still the fourth-largest year on record and comfortably above the 2010–2021 average of 473 tonnes ([World Gold Council](https://www.gold.org/goldhub/research/gold-demand-trends/gold-demand-trends-full-year-2025/central-banks)). Poland's central bank was the single largest buyer, adding 102 tonnes to bring its reserves to 550 tonnes — 28% of its total reserves. Kazakhstan added 57 tonnes, its most in over three decades. Brazil's central bank came back into the market after a four-year absence, buying 43 tonnes. China added 27 tonnes officially for the year, though that number deserves its own paragraph below.

Meanwhile, ETF and bar-and-coin investors — who had mostly sat out the 2022–2023 rally — came back hard. Global gold ETFs added 801.2 tonnes in 2025, a swing of over 800 tonnes from 2024's net outflow of 2.9 tonnes; US-listed funds alone took in 437 tonnes, pushing their holdings to a record 2,019 tonnes. Bar and coin buying hit a 12-year high at 1,374.1 tonnes, up 16% year over year, led by a 29% jump in Greater China and an 85% jump in Europe. Add it up and total investment demand — ETFs plus bars and coins — was 2,175.3 tonnes in 2025, up 84% from 2024 ([World Gold Council](https://www.gold.org/goldhub/research/gold-demand-trends/gold-demand-trends-full-year-2025/investment)). Investment, not central banks, was the single largest driver of 2025's record demand.

The one demand category that actually shrank was jewelry — down 18% in volume to 1,542.3 tonnes, even as its total value rose 18% to a record $172 billion ([World Gold Council](https://www.gold.org/goldhub/research/gold-demand-trends/gold-demand-trends-full-year-2025/jewellery)). People bought less gold and spent more money doing it, which is just what happens when a good triples in price over three years and consumers respond to the sticker, not the ounce.

Then there's the China question. The People's Bank of China has added to its reserves for 20 straight months, reporting official holdings of 2,346 tonnes as of June 2026. Almost nobody in the market believes that's the real number. China is the world's largest gold miner, and a portion of domestic mine output is widely believed to flow directly into state reserves without showing up in trade statistics. Estimates of China's actual holdings run from roughly 5,000 tonnes — Bruce Ikemizu of the Japan Bullion Market Association's estimate, about double the official figure — up to speculative outlier numbers as high as 30,000 tonnes. Société Générale has estimated China's 2025 purchases alone at roughly ten times the officially reported figure ([Discovery Alert](https://discoveryalert.com.au/china-pboc-gold-purchases-central-bank-reserves-2026/)). I'd treat the specific numbers on the high end of that range skeptically — a 2x-to-10x spread between estimates is itself a sign that nobody actually knows. But the direction of the disagreement only goes one way: every credible estimate is higher than the official one, never lower.

## Why it matters

There's a specific event behind this, not a vague mood. In February 2022, the US, EU, and allies froze roughly $300 billion of Russia's foreign exchange reserves in response to the invasion of Ukraine — about half of Russia's total reserves at the time ([CEPR](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/russian-sanctions-and-dollar-foreign-exchange-reserves)). What gets missed in that story is the timing: Russia's central bank had already been shifting reserves out of dollar assets and into gold and yuan since 2014, after the annexation of Crimea and the first round of sanctions, and gold and yuan made up nearly half of its reserves by 2022 ([Mining.com](https://www.mining.com/web/russias-embrace-of-gold-eases-loss-of-reserves-frozen-by-war/)). The freeze didn't start that diversification — it validated it, in the most expensive way possible for every other government watching.

That's the mechanism behind the buying, not a coincidence sitting next to it. In the World Gold Council's 2025 Central Bank Gold Survey, 95% of respondents expected global official gold reserves to keep growing over the next year, and a record 43% said they personally planned to add to their own holdings ([World Gold Council](https://www.gold.org/goldhub/research/gold-demand-trends/gold-demand-trends-full-year-2025/central-banks)). Asked why, central bankers cited reserve diversification, long-term strategic value, and — explicitly, from Poland's governor — national security. A reserve asset that can't be frozen, sanctioned, or unilaterally devalued by another country's monetary policy is worth paying up for if you think you might someday be on the wrong side of a dispute with the country that issues your reserve currency. That calculus applies to a lot more central banks than just Russia's.

This connects directly to price, not just sentiment. Gold's ETF- and futures-driven investor base used to be the swing buyer that set the market price at the margin. Central banks — largely price-insensitive, buying for strategic reasons on multi-year horizons — have taken over that role. That's not a minor detail. It's the reason the next section's math stopped working.

## Gold's fundamentals — and where they've bent

The textbook model of gold pricing runs on four legs: it moves inversely with real interest rates, because gold pays no yield and higher real rates raise the opportunity cost of holding it; it moves inversely with the dollar, because gold is dollar-priced; it acts as an inflation hedge; and it costs money to store and insure, which is a permanent drag with no offsetting income. For two decades, the real-rates relationship in particular held up well — the rolling 12-month correlation between gold and real yields averaged around -0.73 from 2003 to 2022, according to State Street's gold research team ([SSGA](https://www.ssga.com/library-content/assets/pdf/apac/gold/2025/en/us-real-rates-still-matter-for-gold.pdf)). That's a strong, textbook-consistent relationship.

Then 2024 and 2025 happened, and it broke. Gold ripped to repeated new highs while 10-year TIPS yields — the cleanest real-rate proxy — stayed firmly positive, not the deeply negative territory the old model would need to justify a rally of that size. The explanation the market has settled on, and the one I find convincing, is the one from the section above: central bank demand replaced the ETF-flow investor as the marginal price-setter, and that buyer doesn't behave like a hedge fund pricing off the 10-year. It's not trading gold. It's re-weighting a balance sheet on a decade-long view, and it keeps buying through periods when the old model says it shouldn't.

I want to be careful not to overstate this, because the old relationship isn't dead — it's demoted. The pullback from January's $5,589 peak to roughly $4,000–4,100 as of August 4, 2026, tracked a real shift in rate expectations, not a random drawdown. The Fed's July 2026 meeting held rates steady, but with a 9-3 vote where all three dissents wanted a hike rather than a cut ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/29/fed-rate-decision-july-2026.html)), and futures markets were pricing roughly 82% odds of an actual increase in September, as of July 23 ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/23/fed-interest-rate-odds-oil-jobless-claims.html)). Rising inflation expectations and cooling rate-cut odds were the specific, cited reasons gold gave back a fifth of its value in weeks. Real rates still move gold at the margin. They're just moving it around a much higher floor than they used to set on their own, because there's now a second buyer underneath the price who isn't selling on a hawkish Fed statement.

The inflation-hedge leg of the traditional case is weaker than most people assume, and this predates the current cycle. The academic literature is fairly consistent on this: gold is a poor inflation hedge over any horizon that matters to an actual investor — even a full decade counts as "short-term" in this research — and only shows reliable hedging properties over multi-generational spans of a century or more, with a long-run beta to CPI estimated around 1.36 in US data back to 1791 ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/bill_stone/2026/02/22/gold-at-record-highs-a-poor-short-term-inflation-hedge/)). If you bought gold in 2026 specifically to hedge this year's inflation print, the historical record says you're likely to be disappointed on exactly the timeframe you care about. The carry-cost leg hasn't changed at all — gold still produces nothing and still costs something to hold. What's changed is that a large, price-insensitive buyer has been willing to eat that cost anyway, for reasons that have nothing to do with yield.

## Is it actually right to buy gold here?

I'll give a real answer instead of "it depends," because the honest version of "it depends" is still a position — it just requires saying which factors actually decide it.

The bull case is real, not manufactured. Central banks have been net buyers for 15 consecutive years, the last four have all cleared 800 tonnes, and the stated motivation — reducing exposure to a reserve currency that can be frozen by its issuer's foreign policy — is grounded in an event that actually happened, not a hypothetical. That's a structural change in who sets the marginal price, and it's the strongest argument for owning some gold as a small, permanent portfolio position: an asset whose main buyer isn't optimizing for near-term returns and isn't going away because a quarter was disappointing.

The bear case is just as real, and I think it's underweighted in most of the bull commentary as of early August 2026. Gold produces no cash flow, ever — the entire return depends on someone else valuing it more than you paid, which makes it a narrative asset in the same structural sense people criticize other narrative assets for being. Buying it after a 65% single-year gain and a 46-year record run, following a blow-off top that already gave back a quarter of its value in a matter of weeks, is a fundamentally different trade than buying it in 2022 when the central-bank-buying story was new and unpriced. And the pace of that buying is already decelerating — 2025's 863 tonnes was 21% below 2024, and the World Gold Council attributed the slowdown directly to central banks getting more price-sensitive at higher levels. That undercuts the cleanest version of the bull thesis, which depends on treating official-sector demand as effectively infinite and indifferent to price. It isn't. It's large and structural, but it still has a demand curve.

What I actually think: the structural case is real enough to justify a small, boring allocation — the kind you hold through cycles as tail insurance against currency and sovereign-debt stress, not a trade you size up because the 2023–2025 rally went well. What I don't think is that the setup as of August 2026 — a parabolic move, a partially-unwound spike, and a buyer whose own pace of purchases just slowed for the first time in years — is a good entry point for adding meaningfully to that position. Those are two different claims, and conflating them is how people end up buying the top of a real, multi-year structural story. None of this is a recommendation to buy or sell anything — see the disclaimer below, and it applies as much to this post as to the price you saw on the way in.
