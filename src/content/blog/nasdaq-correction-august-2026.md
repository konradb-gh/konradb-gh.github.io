The Nasdaq had a rough week. On July 29, the Nasdaq Composite closed at 24,442 — a 10.1% drop from its June 1 high of 27,190 — which is the technical definition of a correction, and its second one this year. The first came in March, when the index fell 13.4% from an October 2025 high. Two corrections in five months is not normal. But "not normal" and "cause for panic" aren't the same thing, and I think the distinction matters here.

Start with what actually caused it. Chip stocks led the way. Semiconductor and memory names shed more than $1 trillion in combined market value over the week — Nvidia alone lost roughly $238 billion, SK Hynix $176 billion, Samsung $173 billion, Micron $113 billion. A few days earlier, in an earlier leg of the same selloff, Micron had dropped 13% in a single session, Intel fell 9%, and AMD fell 7%. The proximate trigger was SK Hynix's earnings report: record profits, but investors didn't like the signal on high-bandwidth memory production, and that reaction rippled through the rest of the sector.

Underneath the earnings reaction is a bigger story: doubt about whether AI infrastructure spending will generate returns commensurate with what's being spent on it. That doubt got sharper because of a specific, less-discussed wrinkle — circular financing. Nvidia is reportedly weighing up to $600 billion in financing tied to OpenAI: roughly $250 billion to guarantee an Ohio data center project and $350 billion to help OpenAI buy Nvidia chips. That's on top of more than $540 billion in similar deals Nvidia has already announced this year, including a $500 billion AI initiative with SK Hynix. The concern isn't that these deals are fake — it's that when a chipmaker finances its own customers' purchases of its own chips, both the revenue and the risk are more concentrated than the headline numbers suggest. If AI demand disappoints, the losses don't stay contained to one company's balance sheet.

Then there's the Fed, which showed up at exactly the wrong moment for a market already nervous about valuations. On the same day the Nasdaq confirmed its correction, the FOMC held rates at 3.50%–3.75% — but by a 9-3 vote, with all three dissents pushing for a hike, not a cut. The 10-year Treasury yield jumped 7 basis points to above 4.67% on the news, and futures markets are now pricing roughly 82% odds of an actual hike in September. Higher rates hit high-multiple growth and AI stocks hardest, so a market already digesting an AI-valuation scare got a second shove from the rate side on the same day.

Does this warrant panic? No — and the market's own recent behavior backs that up. The Nasdaq bounced 2.8% the very next session, closing at 25,122 and ending a six-day losing streak. By the following Monday, the Nasdaq-100 was up another 1.8% intraday and the semiconductor index closed up over 1%. That's a fast recovery, but it's not an unusual one — the average pullback in the 10%-to-20% range has historically taken about four months to fully recover, and the Nasdaq has posted a positive return in the 12 months following 11 of its last 12 corrections. This one is also shallower than March's: 10.1% versus 13.4%. And here's the detail I find most telling — the low of this correction, 24,442, sits above the high that preceded the March correction, 24,019. Each pullback this cycle has bottomed higher than the last one peaked. That's what a correction inside an intact uptrend looks like, not the early stage of something worse.

Where I'd push back on pure reassurance is this: the Fed piece of this story is the normal, well-understood kind of risk. Markets have repriced rate expectations a hundred times before and mostly muddled through. The circular financing piece is not that. It's a newer structure, concentrated among a handful of counterparties, and it hasn't been stress-tested by an actual AI demand shortfall yet. If that spending disappoints, a web of vendor financing that inflates revenue on the way up can unwind faster than a garden-variety valuation correction unwinds on the way down. That's the scenario that would make this correction behave differently from the historical base rate — not the Fed meeting everyone's watching.

The next real test isn't September — it's August 27, when Nvidia reports earnings and Wall Street finds out whether roughly 53% year-over-year revenue growth is still on the table. If it is, this correction probably finishes looking like every other one this cycle: a sharp, fast pullback that bottoms above the last peak and recovers within a few months. If it isn't, the circular financing story stops being a theoretical risk and starts being the headline.
