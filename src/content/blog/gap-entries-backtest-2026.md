Two things can be true about a trading result at the same time, and they sound like they should be the same fact when they aren't. A signal can be statistically real — meaning it beats random chance by more than luck alone should allow — and still be something nobody could ever actually trade. And a result that "holds up" when you check it separately in two different time periods looks like strong, independent evidence. It doesn't have to be. Both halves can be propped up by their own separate stroke of luck, and checking twice won't catch that on its own. This experiment produced a clean, fully-documented example of both problems — from my own trading-research repo, [Experiment 06](https://github.com/konradb-gh/trading-research/tree/main/experiments/06-gap-entries), specifically.

Quick refresher if you're coming in cold: trades in this research are measured in **R** (your result as a multiple of what you risked) and **expectancy** (the average R you make per trade, across everything). I define both from scratch in the [Experiment 05 post](/blog/stop-fill-assumption-backtest-2026); this one assumes you've got the basic idea and moves faster.

## What a gap is, and three ways to trade one

A **gap** is what happens when a stock's price jumps overnight instead of moving gradually during the trading day. Say a stock closes Tuesday at $50. Overnight, the company beats earnings estimates, and when the market reopens Wednesday, the very first trade isn't anywhere near $50 — it's $54. That $4 jump between one day's close and the next day's open, with no trading in between, is the gap.

Gaps are dramatic, visible, and happen for an identifiable reason — news — which makes them an obvious thing to try to build a strategy around. This experiment tested three ways to do it:

- **Continuation** — the stock gaps up; you buy it, betting the move keeps running.
- **Fade** — the stock gaps up, then cools off a little; you buy that small pullback, betting the original move resumes.
- **Bounce** — the stock gaps down; you buy it, betting on a reversal back up.

Only continuation is a genuinely new idea. Fade is the system's existing "buy a dip inside an uptrend" setup with a gap supplying the excuse; bounce is the same short-term oversold-bounce idea [Experiment 01](/blog/connors-dip-buying-indices-2026) already tested on indices, just triggered by a gap instead of an RSI reading.

## Two quick, boring failures

Fade failed everywhere. Every configuration tested — every gap size, every added filter, every way of exiting the trade — came back negative. Not "mostly negative": all 48 combinations tested, zero winners, best result anywhere a loss of −0.085R. There's nothing to dig into; the idea just doesn't work.

Bounce looked more promising in isolation — its best version returned a real positive number. It failed anyway, against something called a **matched control**, and the concept is worth understanding because it comes back later for the interesting result too. A positive return by itself doesn't prove a strategy is doing anything: stocks go up more often than they go down, so almost any reasonably sensible way of buying them will show a positive number just from riding that general drift. A matched control asks a sharper question: does this specific rule beat what you'd get from randomly buying similar stocks on the same days? Bounce's best setup returned +0.633% per trade. Random stocks that passed the same basic quality checks, bought on those same down-gap days, returned +0.680%. Random did as well or better. The gap wasn't adding anything — those were just good days to own stocks in general.

One more detail worth sitting with. Bounce has an optional "quality filter" — a check for whether the stock is in a reasonably healthy uptrend before you're allowed to buy its dip. Turning that filter off didn't just make bounce a little worse. It flipped the result to a clear, statistically decisive loss — down to −0.64R in the worst configurations, with a t-statistic (a measure of how confidently a result can be said to differ from pure noise; roughly, further from zero means less room for coincidence) as extreme as −7.5, leaving essentially no room for coincidence. Buying stocks that just cratered *and* are already in a downtrend is a reliable way to lose money, which is a useful thing to have actually measured rather than assumed.

## Continuation: the one that took real work to kill

This is the interesting one, and the only genuinely new idea in the batch. At a 2% up-gap, no extra filters, holding for 10 trading sessions: expectancy of **+0.0373R** across **1,443 trades**. It's positive in both halves of the test period separately (+0.0354R and +0.0392R). It beats its matched random control resoundingly — better than 100% of 103 random comparison draws measured in R, and better than 98% of them measured in plain percentage terms. On a first read, that's about as good as this kind of test gets.

It doesn't survive a closer look, and the closer look is simple: what does the *typical* trade in this group actually look like, not just the average?

The median trade — the one right in the middle if you lined up all 1,443 trades from worst to best — **loses** money, at −0.171R. The average is positive only because one single trade in the group returned **+101.79R**, a wildly outsized result. Drop that one trade — one out of 1,443 — and the whole group's average flips to **−0.0332R**, a loser.

Think of it like a coin you're testing for a hidden bias. You flip it a thousand times and it comes up heads 51% of the time — mildly interesting. Then you notice one of those "heads" was, by a scoring mistake, recorded as worth 100 points instead of 1. Take that single flip back out, score it correctly, and your whole "the coin favors heads" conclusion evaporates. That's exactly what's happening here: one abnormal outcome, sitting inside 1,442 completely ordinary ones, is doing all the work.

That should also make you suspicious of the "positive in both halves" check that looked so reassuring a moment ago — and it should be, though not in quite the same way in each half. In the second half of the test period, it really is one trade: that same outsized win sits there, and removing just it flips the second half's average from +0.0392R to **−0.1021R**. The first half is a little more diffuse but no more real — a single trade doesn't rescue it, but removing its entire top 1% of trades (about seven out of roughly 720) does flip it negative too. Both halves "passed." Neither survives losing its own best few trades.

There's a second reason not to fully trust the headline number, and it's almost stranger than the first. This system sizes each position based on how tight its stop-loss is — a trade with a very close stop gets a bigger position, because the dollar amount at risk is meant to stay the same either way. That's normally sensible. But it means a single trade with an unusually tight stop that happens to recover can register as a triple-digit "R" figure, even though the actual dollar amount involved was completely ordinary — R is a ratio to that one trade's own stop distance, not a fixed unit. Measure the exact same 1,443 trades in plain percentage of the account instead of in R, and the result doesn't just shrink. It goes negative, to **−0.090%**. The published +0.0373R and the real −0.090% describe the identical set of trades. Only one of those two numbers is telling you what actually happened to your money.

One fair note before moving on: continuation genuinely does beat random selection, on both the R basis and the percentage basis, and that part isn't an artifact — the gap trigger is carrying *some* real information about which stocks are about to move. "Beats random" and "is a tradeable edge" are different claims, though, and only the first one holds up here.

**The lesson this produced, stated as a standing rule going forward:** checking two time periods separately isn't enough on its own, if a single lucky trade can carry an entire period by itself. You also have to check whether the result survives losing its own single best trade — in the pooled sample and in each half separately — before "it held up" means anything.

## The impossible best case, and what killed it anyway

There's a special kind of test worth defining before showing the result, because it sounds like cheating and is used on purpose: a **look-ahead bound**. It's a deliberately impossible, best-case version of a strategy — imagine having a magic ability to know, an instant before the market opens, exactly how far a stock is about to gap down, and being able to buy it at that exact reopening price. Nobody can do that in real life. The point of testing the impossible version first is that it sets a ceiling: if even the version with a perfect, instant, friction-free fill doesn't produce anything worth having, the real version — where a real trader is necessarily a step slower — definitely won't either.

For the down-gap bounce, one correction had to come first: **survivorship bias**. Older test data tends to only include companies that are still around today, because the historical list of "who was in the S&P 500" is usually pulled from a current source. That quietly deletes the companies that later went bankrupt or got bought out, which flatters any strategy tested on the surviving names, since the failures have been erased from the sample. Once that's corrected — testing against the actual index membership as it stood on each historical day, failed companies included — the impossible best-case version of down-gap bounce actually looked good: buying the stock at the very instant of the gap beat about 98% of random, similarly-selected comparison trades.

Then comes the twist. Switch to the version anyone could actually trade — waiting until the *next* morning's open, because that's the earliest moment a real order could physically be filled, since a system that scans once a day before the market opens can only react to a gap after it has already happened — and the result doesn't just get a little worse. It flips to *worse than random chance entirely*. Instead of beating 98% of random comparison trades, it now beats only about 4.5% of them. It doesn't just lose its edge; it loses outright to a coin flip — the random control made +0.533% per trade over those same days, while the real, tradeable version lost −0.050%.

Here's a real-life version of why. Imagine driving past a shop with a flash-sale sign in the window — the price looks incredible for the split second you see it. You can't stop the car in time, but you make a note to come back first thing tomorrow. By the time you arrive the next morning, the sale ended overnight. Whatever made that price attractive was gone by the time your actual, physically possible opportunity to act on it arrived. That's the entire down-gap reversal effect in one sentence: it's real, and it lives in the opening auction — the first few minutes of trading, while a gap's price is still being set — and it is almost completely gone one trading session later, which is the earliest a real, pre-open daily scanner could ever show up to buy it.

There's one more layer, and it makes even the impossible version worse. Trading into a volatile, wide-open gap costs more than trading normally — wider spreads, faster-moving prices, more slippage. Modeling that extra cost (roughly 50 cents per $100 traded on gapping days, instead of the usual 10 cents) knocks the impossible best-case version down further: from beating 98.0% of random trades to beating only 88.5% of them — below the 95% bar the paper requires before calling something a real effect. A version using a different exit rule shows the identical pattern, dropping from 99.5% to 89.0%.

The paper's own title says all of this in eight words: *"An Edge You Cannot Reach, and Could Not Afford."* The "cannot reach" part is the overnight-hold problem — the effect is real but gone before a real trader could arrive. The "could not afford" part is the extra cost of trading into a gap, which weakens the result even in the impossible version that could theoretically be reached. Either one alone would be enough to kill the idea. Both are true at once.

## A bug that has nothing to do with gaps, and matters more

While chasing all of the above, the researchers found something that isn't really about gap trading at all — and it arguably matters more than any single result in this paper.

Tests like this need to know, for any given day in history, exactly which companies were in the S&P 500 on that day — index membership changes constantly as companies get added, removed, acquired, or go bankrupt. The tooling had been answering that question by asking a public source (Wikipedia) for the *current* list every single time a test ran, rather than locking in what the list actually was on the historical dates being tested. That current list had quietly changed — one company, EA (Electronic Arts), had dropped off it — since the very first experiments in this series were originally run.

That sounds like it should be a rounding error. It isn't, for a specific reason worth spelling out. Some of the system's calculations don't look at one stock in isolation — they rank a stock's strength *relative to every other stock in the index*, or count what percentage of the whole index is currently in an uptrend. Remove one company from that comparison group, and it can shift every other company's ranking slightly. In the thinner, smaller stock universe of the early 1990s, that small shift was enough to flip some borderline days from "pass" to "fail," or back.

What this means in practice: someone trying to exactly recreate this project's earlier published results from scratch today, using the same published instructions, would get a very slightly different answer than what was originally published — through no fault of the original work. The fix is simple to describe: instead of asking "who's in the index today" every time a test runs, the tooling now locks in a permanent, frozen snapshot of exactly who was in the index on each specific historical date being tested, once, so the answer can never quietly drift again. Checking, the drift turned out to be small in dollar terms — in one earlier experiment it affected 21 trades out of 4,496 and moved a published result by about a thousandth of a percentage point (+0.0012pp, to be exact) — but the size of the mistake isn't really the point. Nobody would have caught it without deliberately trying to rebuild an old result from scratch and checking whether the new version matched to the decimal.

One more thing came out of chasing this down, and it's a useful lesson on its own: two of this project's earlier experiments had published reproduction instructions that, it turned out, didn't actually work as written the moment someone finally ran them command-for-command instead of just reading them and assuming they were fine. Writing down the steps to reproduce a result isn't the same as verifying that those exact steps reproduce it — and this project only learned that by finally checking.

## The verdict

None of the three gap strategies are joining the live trading system. Fade lost everywhere, with nothing left to debate. Bounce, in the version anyone could actually trade, loses to random chance outright, and turning off its one safety filter makes it lose in a much bigger and much more statistically certain way. Continuation, the only genuinely new idea of the three, is the one that mattered most to test carefully — it looked real, survived being split into two separate time periods, and beat random selection convincingly. It was still an illusion, built mostly on a couple of freakishly lucky trades sitting inside an otherwise ordinary, slightly-losing set of results.

That's the single most useful, exportable lesson in this whole experiment — more useful than any one of the three results on its own. A result "holding up" across two different time periods sounds like real, independent evidence. It isn't automatically. Both periods can be propped up by their own separate stroke of luck, and the only way to know is to check whether either period's result survives losing its own best trade. "It worked in two different eras" is not the same claim as "it will keep working," and this experiment is a clean, fully-documented example of exactly how those two claims come apart.

The process worked exactly as it was built to. It went in testing a fairly narrow question — do gaps make a good entry signal — and came out having also caught a structural bug quietly sitting underneath years of this project's earlier, already-published results. That's not a consolation prize for three failed strategies. Catching an invisible problem before it corrupts the next round of experiments is a better outcome than finding a tradeable gap strategy would have been, and it's the kind of result that only shows up when you're willing to actually re-run your own published instructions and check the number instead of trusting that it still holds.
